01 The raw dataset

Diabetes 130-US Hospitals (1999-2008) was extracted from the Health Facts clinical warehouse (Cerner) and covers ten years of inpatient care across 130 hospitals and integrated delivery networks in the United States. It was assembled for a specific question - does measuring HbA1c on admission change the odds of readmission? - which is why its columns lean toward labs, medications, and follow-up. It holds 101,766 encounters belonging to 71,518 distinct patients (so a patient can recur), described by 50 fields.

It is already a filtered cohort

A row survives into the file only if all five hold - worth knowing, because it means the population is pre-selected, not a random slice of hospital admissions:

  • the encounter is an inpatient admission;
  • it is a diabetic encounter - diabetes appears in one of the three diagnosis codes;
  • length of stay is 1-14 days (mean 4.4);
  • lab tests were performed during the stay;
  • medications were administered during the stay.

The label is three-way, not binary: no readmission, a late readmission (>30 days), or an early one (<30 days). Only the last is the clinically and financially costly event, so we collapse it to Y = 1{not readmitted within 30 days} - higher is better.

Data quirks that shape the design. Three fields are largely empty - weight (97%), medical_specialty (49%), payer_code (40%) - so we don't build on them. Labs are ordered selectively, making "None" missing-not-at-random rather than a true value. Coded ID fields need the mapping file to mean anything. And because the same patient recurs, rows aren't independent - which is exactly why cleaning (next) de-duplicates to one stay per patient.

02 Cleaning

Two standard steps before anything else:

  • Drop encounters where readmission is undefined - discharges to expired or hospice (discharge_disposition_id in {11,13,14,19,20,21}); a deceased patient must not count as "not readmitted."
  • De-duplicate to one encounter per patient (first stay by patient_nbr) so rows are independent.

-> 69,990 patients; real 30-day readmission rate 9.0%.

03 The split: what the model sees, and what it doesn't

The whole point of a confounding experiment is a variable that drives both treatment and outcome but is hidden from the estimator. We carve the real columns into an observed covariate and a withheld confounder - both from real data, deliberately kept distinct.

Observed · X

A frailty risk score

Standardized sum of five things a clinician readily sees, rescaled to [−1,1]:

  • age bracket
  • num_medications
  • time_in_hospital (length of stay)
  • num_lab_procedures, num_procedures
Withheld · S in {-1, +1}

True acuity (the confounder)

A distinct severity signal held out from the model - median-split into "sicker / not":

  • number_inpatient + number_emergency (prior-year acute use)
  • number_diagnoses (comorbidity burden)
  • discharge to a skilled facility

X and S share no columns, but both reflect underlying sickness - so they correlate, exactly as a real observed proxy correlates with an unmeasured one.

04 The real coupling - the part we don't invent

This is the crux. Because sicker patients are older, on more drugs, and stay longer, the observed score X partially predicts the hidden acuity S. That relationship - P(S=+1∣X) - is measured straight from the data, not modeled:

Why r≈0.20 is the whole story. X is a partial proxy for S. Balancing the observed X therefore partially balances the hidden S - that residual leverage is exactly what the Wasserstein (O-W) methods exploit. But the coupling is far from perfect, so unobserved confounding remains - and that residual is what the Γ sensitivity model is there to hedge. On our synthetic owgap DGP the coupling was near-deterministic; here it is honestly weak, which is the real-data challenge.

05 The confounding structure

The hidden acuity S sits on both arrows into the decision and the outcome; the observed X only correlates with it. Standard IPW/AIPW, blind to S, are biased; the robust methods hedge that bias.

06 The semi-synthetic layer

On top of the real (X, S) pairs we synthesize the treatment and both potential outcomes - the only way to have ground-truth counterfactuals (and thus an oracle and a true realized E[Y]) that a purely observational dataset can never give.

Treatment - confounded, at a controllable strength

Now using the real treatment. Per your call, T is taken straight from the data - T = 1{insulin != No} (the decision plotted earlier) - rather than sampled. The confounded propensity form below is kept as the synthetic-T alternative; with a real T the outcomes can be left real (fully observational, no oracle) or still synthesized, which we lock next.
T~Bernoulli(e(X,S)),e(X,S)=clip(σ(cSS−cXX), 0.02, 0.98)

Sicker patients (S=+1) are escalated more often; cS sets the matched sensitivity Γ=e2cS≈3 - the true odds-ratio a Γ-model would need to see. This is the knob that makes the confounding real but bounded.

Outcomes - acuity dominates, the effect flips across X

Y(t)=μt(X,S)+ε,μ0=d0S,μ1=d1S+b1X,ε~𝒩(0,σ2)

Calibrated so |d0|, |d1|≫|b1| - the hidden acuity swamps the outcome scale (mirroring the real 13.8% vs 7.4% readmission gap by S) - while the treatment effect CATE(X)=(d1−d0)E^[S∣X]+b1X changes sign across X, so the optimal policy treats only part of the population (a real decision, not "treat everyone").

Why not fully observational? The real data gives you one factual outcome per patient - no counterfactual, so no oracle and no true realized E[Y] to score against. By synthesizing outcomes on real covariates and a real withheld confounder, we keep the entire evaluation machinery (oracle, realized E[Y], Γ/ε sweeps) working, while the confounding strength Γ becomes a dial we set rather than a mystery we guess.

07 What this plugs into

The result is a drop-in DGP with the same generate(n, seed) -> (observed, full) contract as the synthetic experiments - so it flows straight through everything already built: the continuous-X pipeline, the fine-mesh training, the Lipschitz policy class, and the KNN / Shapley off-support extension. Each run lands as its own tab in the report, next to owgap.

The question it answers: when the covariate-confounder coupling is honestly weak (r ~ 0.20, not the near-deterministic owgap link), does covariate-balancing (O-W) plus a Γ hedge still beat naive IPW / AIPW on real clinical covariates?
